# Chapter 16 — Vision and Multimodal Transformers, to fall asleep to

Chapter sixteen. Vision and multimodal transformers.

---

Beyond text. Transformers are exceptionally good at processing not only text but images, audio, video, robot sensor data, and almost any other kind of input. The chapter has two parts. The first covers vision transformers, which process images: DETR, the original ViT, DeiT, PVT, Swin and DINO. The second covers multimodal transformers, which handle several kinds of input at once: VideoBERT, ViLBERT, CLIP, DALL-E, the Perceiver, Perceiver IO, Flamingo and BLIP-2.

---

Recurrent neural networks with visual attention. One of the first uses of attention outside natural language processing was image captioning. A convolutional neural network, or CNN, processes the image and outputs feature maps, then a decoder recurrent neural network, or RNN, with an attention mechanism generates the caption one token at a time. At each step, attention focuses on the relevant part of the image. When such a model captioned a photo as a woman throwing a Frisbee in a park, most of its attention was on the Frisbee just before it output the word Frisbee.

Attention also shows what led a model to its output. This is called explainability, and it is especially useful when the model makes a mistake. If a dog walking in the snow is captioned as a wolf walking in the snow, checking where the model looked when it output the word wolf may show it attended to the snow as well as the dog. The model may have learned to tell dogs from wolves by the presence of snow, which more training images of wolves without snow and dogs with snow would fix. In some applications, such as a system deciding whether to grant a loan, explainability is a legal requirement.

Once transformers were invented, they quickly replaced the RNNs in existing vision architectures such as captioning systems. A CNN still did most of the visual work, so these models are not usually counted as vision transformers.

---

DETR, the detection transformer. In May 2020, Facebook researchers proposed DETR, a hybrid CNN and transformer for object detection. A CNN turns the input image into feature maps, the feature maps become a sequence of visual tokens, and an encoder-decoder transformer outputs a sequence of bounding box predictions. A few months later, the CNN was removed entirely.

---

The original vision transformer. In October 2020, Google researchers introduced the first vision transformer with no CNN, named simply the vision transformer, or ViT. The image is chopped into patches of sixteen by sixteen pixels, and the sequence of patches is treated like a sequence of word representations. The paper's title makes the point: an image is worth sixteen by sixteen words.

Each patch is flattened into a vector of sixteen times sixteen times three values, which is seven hundred sixty-eight, the three being the red, green and blue channels. A two hundred twenty-four pixel square image becomes a fourteen by fourteen grid, so one hundred ninety-six such vectors. A linear layer projects each vector to the transformer's embedding size. From there, the sequence is handled exactly like word embeddings: learnable positional embeddings are added, and the result goes through a regular encoder-only transformer. A trainable class token is inserted at the start of the sequence, and a classification head sits on top of its output, as in BERT.

ViT beat the state of the art on ImageNet classification, but the authors used over three hundred million additional training images. Transformers have fewer inductive biases than CNNs, so they need extra data to learn what CNNs assume implicitly.

An inductive bias is an implicit assumption a model makes because of its architecture. Linear models assume the data is linear. CNNs are translation invariant, so they assume a pattern learned in one place is useful elsewhere, and they have a strong bias toward locality. RNNs assume inputs are ordered and that recent tokens matter more than older ones. The more inductive biases a model has, the less data it needs, provided the assumptions are correct. If they are wrong, the model may perform poorly even on a large dataset.

---

Implementing and fine-tuning a ViT. In PyTorch, the patch embedding can be a single two-dimensional convolutional layer whose kernel size and stride both equal the patch size, which is equivalent to chopping the image into patches, flattening them and applying a linear layer. The output is flattened and transposed so the embedding dimension comes last. The model adds a trainable class token and trainable positional embeddings, one more than the number of patches, both initialized from a normal distribution with a small standard deviation such as zero point zero two. It runs a standard transformer encoder with the GELU activation, keeps only the class token's output, normalizes it, and passes it through a linear classification head. The base configuration has twelve layers, twelve attention heads and an embedding size of seven hundred sixty-eight. It trains with the cross-entropy loss, but training from scratch is slow, so unless the dataset is very domain-specific, fine-tuning a pretrained ViT is usually better.

The book's example fine-tunes a base ViT, pretrained on the fourteen million images of ImageNet-21k, on the Oxford-IIIT Pet dataset: over seven thousand pet pictures in thirty-seven classes. The Transformers library's ViT for image classification class replaces the original head with a new, untrained thirty-seven class head. The model's image processor resizes images to two hundred twenty-four pixels square, scales pixel values to between minus one and one, and puts the channel dimension first. A data collator processes each batch and forces conversion to RGB, because some images have a transparency channel that would cause an error mid-training. The Hugging Face trainer must be told not to remove unused columns, because it otherwise drops the image column before the collator sees it. After three epochs, accuracy is about ninety-two percent, and ninety-three to ninety-five percent is reachable with augmentation and more training.

---

The data-efficient image transformer. Two months after ViT, Facebook researchers released DeiT, which achieved competitive ImageNet results with no additional training data. Its architecture is almost identical to ViT. The difference is distillation from a teacher model.

The teacher is a frozen, state-of-the-art CNN. A special distillation token is added to the student ViT. Like the class token, it is trainable and has its own classification head. Both heads are trained with the cross-entropy loss, but the class token's head uses the usual hard targets, one-hot vectors, while the distillation head uses the soft targets output by the teacher. The final loss is a weighted sum of the two, typically with equal weights. At inference time, the distillation token and its head are dropped. A distilled DeiT fine-tuned on the same pets dataset reaches about ninety-four percent accuracy after three epochs.

---

The pyramid vision transformer. ViT and DeiT suit classification, but dense prediction tasks, such as object detection and semantic segmentation, need fine-grained spatial resolution. In February 2021, researchers from Nanjing University and several partners released the pyramid vision transformer, or PVT. Like a CNN, it turns the image into gradually smaller but deeper, semantically richer images. A two hundred fifty-six by one hundred ninety-two RGB image becomes sixty-four by forty-eight with sixty-four channels, then thirty-two by twenty-four with one hundred twenty-eight channels, then sixteen by twelve with three hundred twenty, and finally eight by six with five hundred twelve.

Each level works like a ViT: the input is chopped into patches, positional embeddings are added, and an encoder processes the tokens. The output sequence is reshaped into a grid and treated as a many-channel image for the next level. The patches are tiny: four by four pixels at the first level, two by two after that. Small patches give high resolution, but many more tokens, and attention cost grows quadratically with the number of tokens.

The fix is spatial reduction attention: ordinary multi-head attention, except that the keys and values are spatially shrunk first, usually by a strided convolution followed by layer normalization, though some implementations use average pooling, while the queries are not. At the first level, a sixty-four by forty-eight grid of four by four patches gives three thousand seventy-two tokens, so full attention would compute over nine million scores. Shrinking the keys and values by a factor of eight in each direction leaves an eight by six grid of forty-eight tokens, so only about one hundred forty-seven thousand scores are needed, sixty-four times fewer. The reduction factor is four, two and one at the next three levels. Because the queries are untouched, the encoder still outputs three thousand seventy-two tokens, and the output resolution stays the same.

PVT's four multiscale feature maps simply replace the CNN backbone in existing dense prediction systems. For semantic segmentation, a fully convolutional network upscales and combines them and outputs one class per pixel. For object detection and instance segmentation, a Mask R-CNN can use a PVT backbone. PVT was a milestone, but it is still computationally expensive.

---

The Swin Transformer. In March 2021, Microsoft researchers released the Swin Transformer. Like PVT, it is hierarchical. But it uses window-based multi-head self-attention: each patch attends only to patches in its own window, so the cost scales linearly with image area instead of quadratically.

At the first level, patches are usually four by four pixels, and each nonoverlapping window holds seven by seven patches. On a twenty-eight by twenty-eight grid of patches, each of the seven hundred eighty-four tokens attends to only forty-nine, for about thirty-eight thousand scores in total instead of over six hundred thousand. Doubling the image's width and height multiplies Swin's cost by four. For ViT, DeiT and PVT, it multiplies the cost by sixteen, which makes them too slow for large images, so those must be downsampled, at some cost in accuracy.

Windows alone cannot capture long-range patterns. Swin stands for shifted windows: every other layer offsets the windows by half a window, three patches toward the bottom right, so patches that were in separate windows can now see each other. Alternating the two allows information to spread across the whole image, and faster at coarser levels of the pyramid. Shifting the windows naively creates extra windows, for example twenty-five instead of sixteen. Instead, the image itself is shifted and wrapped around the borders, which keeps sixteen windows, and an attention mask stops wrapped regions that were not neighbors from attending to each other.

Swin is harder to implement than PVT, but its linear scaling and excellent performance make it one of the best vision transformers. Swin version two, from November 2021, brought more stable training for large models, easier fine-tuning on large images, and less need for labeled data. It is still widely used.

---

DINO. In April 2021, Mathilde Caron and colleagues introduced DINO, short for self-distillation with no labels, a self-supervised technique that produces excellent image representations.

The model is duplicated into a teacher and a student. Gradient descent updates only the student. The teacher's weights are an exponential moving average of the student's, which is called a momentum teacher. The student learns to match the teacher's predictions, and since the two are almost the same model, this is self-distillation.

Each image is augmented with color jitter, grayscale, blur, flipping and more, differently for each network. The teacher sees the full image, lightly augmented. The student often sees only a zoomed-in crop, more strongly augmented. Their predictions must still match, which forces agreement on high-level content.

This risks mode collapse, where both networks output the same thing regardless of the input. Centering prevents one form of it: a moving average of the teacher's logits is subtracted from them, which spreads its predictions evenly across classes over time. But centering alone could make the teacher output the same probability for every class, so sharpening is also applied: the teacher's logits are divided by a temperature below one, forcing confident top predictions. Together they keep the outputs diverse, so the model must rely on the image's content.

After training, the teacher is dropped and the student is the DINO model. A classifier head can be trained on its class token output. Even simpler, averaging the class token representations of the training images per class, then assigning a new image to the nearest class average, reaches about seventy-eight percent top-one accuracy on ImageNet.

DINO's last-layer attention maps often outline the main object in the image, though the model never saw a label, and each attention head tends to focus on a different part of the object. Later work such as TokenCut used this to segment objects in images and video. Meta's DINO version two, from April 2023, was trained on a larger curated dataset and outputs per-patch features, which makes it a strong foundation model for dense prediction as well as classification.

Other vision models and techniques. A June 2021 Google paper showed how to scale ViTs to the available data: a two billion parameter model reached over ninety percent top-one accuracy on ImageNet, while a small one reached about eighty-five percent from only ten images per class.

BEiT, also from June 2021, introduced masked image modeling, inspired by BERT's masked language modeling: the model reconstructs masked patches from visible ones. It does not predict pixels. A discrete variational autoencoder maps each patch to a visual token ID from a fixed vocabulary, and BEiT predicts those IDs, so its capacity is not wasted on unnecessary detail.

Masked autoencoders, from Facebook researchers led by Kaiming He in November 2021, predict raw pixels directly instead. A large encoder sees only the visible patches, and a lightweight decoder reconstructs the whole image. Seventy-five percent of patches are masked, so pretraining is cheap enough for very large datasets.

Model soups, from 2022, average the weights of several trained transformers into one improved model, like an ensemble with no extra inference cost. EVA is a family of large ViTs pretrained at scale with an enhanced masked autoencoder approach; EVA-02's large variant reaches ninety percent on ImageNet with three hundred four million parameters.

JEPA, the joint-embedding predictive architecture, was proposed by Yann LeCun in 2022 as part of his world-model framework. I-JEPA applies it to images, and V-JEPA to video. A teacher encoder sees the full input, such as a photo of a cat, and a student encoder sees only part of it, such as the cat without its ears. A predictor uses the student's embeddings to predict the teacher's embedding of the missing part. The teacher is a moving average of the student, as in DINO. Working in embedding space instead of pixel space makes JEPA fast, parameter efficient and more semantic. Only the student encoder is kept.

Many other vision transformers exist, including small, efficient models for mobile devices, hierarchical backbones for dense prediction, segmentation models such as SAM for interactive segmentation, and models for object detection and video understanding. Efficiency techniques include token merging, token pruning of low-attention tokens, early exiting, and selecting only the most informative patches.

Multimodal machine learning. Humans perceive the world through many senses and act on it through movement, speech and writing. Modalities are heterogeneous: continuous or discrete, temporal or spatial, high resolution like forty-eight kilohertz audio or low resolution like text, noisy or clean. They also interact. Lip movements carry information that overlaps with speech. Combined modalities can create new meaning: saying he's an expert while rolling the eyes inverts the sentence and adds humor or disdain that neither modality carries alone.

Multimodal learning faces two main challenges. Fusion is combining modalities, for example by encoding them into the same representation space. Alignment is discovering how they relate, such as finding the timestamp of each word in a recording given its transcript, or finding the object an expression like the dog next to the tree refers to, which is called visual grounding. Other multimodal tasks include image captioning, image search, visual question answering, speech-to-text, text-to-speech, and embodied AI, models that physically interact with their environment.

Transformers fit well. Any modality that can be chopped into meaningful tokens can be fed in: text into words, images into patches, audio or video into short clips. Embeddings from different modalities can be summed, concatenated or passed through a fusion encoder, which handles fusion, and multi-head attention finds patterns within and across modalities, which handles alignment. The first multimodal transformers appeared within months of the original Transformer paper, for image and video captioning.

---

VideoBERT. In April 2019, Google researchers released VideoBERT. They took a pretrained BERT-large model, extended its embedding matrix with video tokens, and kept training it with self-supervision on instructional YouTube videos, mostly cooking videos where someone describes actions while doing them.

The video side was cut into one and a half second clips, each passed through S3D, a three-dimensional CNN pretrained on videos of human actions, and pooled into a single vector of one thousand twenty-four dimensions. The text came from YouTube's speech-to-text software, was split into sentences by an off-the-shelf long short-term memory model, and was tokenized as for BERT.

Masked language modeling predicts tokens from a fixed vocabulary, and video clips have none, so the authors built one with hierarchical k-means. They clustered all clip vectors into twelve clusters, split each cluster into twelve, and repeated until there were twelve to the fourth power, about twenty thousand seven hundred clusters. This is faster than one big k-means and usually gives better clusters. Each clip became its cluster ID, so a video became a sequence of visual token IDs, like tokenized text. Going from a continuous space to about twenty thousand discrete values loses a lot of information, but VideoBERT's performance suggests much of the important information survives. The text embeddings came from BERT-large and were already excellent. The visual token embeddings were frozen and initialized with the cluster centroids.

There were three separate training regimes. In text-only and video-only modes, the model predicted masked tokens. In text plus video mode, the two sequences were concatenated, and a binary head on the class token predicted whether they came from the same part of the video. This is linguistic-visual alignment, and it replaces BERT's next sentence prediction. Negative examples paired random sentences with random video segments. Because cooks often describe what they did earlier or will do later, neighboring sentences were concatenated for context, and the video sampling rate was varied randomly so the model would handle cooks of different speeds.

For zero-shot action classification, the video is fed with the sentence now let me show you how to, a masked verb, the word the, and a masked noun. For a cook slicing tomatoes, slice and tomatoes get far higher probabilities than bake and cake. For captioning, VideoBERT's outputs replaced the encoder input of an earlier captioning transformer, which improved its results. For multiple-choice questions, each answer is scored by its alignment with the video.

VideoBERT inspired many BERT-based multimodal models in 2019. Most, like VideoBERT, are single-stream: modalities are fused early, usually by concatenation. ViLBERT and LXMERT are dual-stream: each modality has its own encoder, and the encoders influence each other, so each modality is understood before their interactions are.

ViLBERT. ViLBERT, the visio-linguistic BERT, was proposed in August 2019 by researchers from Georgia Tech, Facebook AI Research and Oregon State University. They argued that the two modalities need different amounts of processing: visual features from a deep CNN are already high level, while text needs much more. They also suspected that image regions relate to each other more weakly than words do, and that forcing a text-only BERT to process images could damage its weights.

So each modality gets its own encoder, and in the upper layers the encoders exchange information through co-attention, a two-way cross-attention: in each connected pair of layers, each encoder attends to keys and values from the other.

The text stream is a twelve-layer BERT base topped by six co-attention layers. The visual features come from a frozen, pretrained Faster R-CNN object detector and are assumed to be high level enough, so the visual stream is only the six matching co-attention layers. The detector's outputs are mean-pooled per detected region into one feature vector, and low-confidence regions are dropped, leaving ten to thirty-six vectors per image. Regions have no natural order, so instead of positional encodings, each region gets a spatial encoding: a five-dimensional vector holding the normalized upper-left and lower-right corners of its bounding box and the fraction of the image the box covers, projected linearly to the feature size and added to the region's features. A special image token is prepended to the visual sequence. It plays the role of the class token, but rather than being trainable, it is the average of the region features plus the spatial encoding of a box covering the whole image.

Text training uses ordinary masked language modeling. There is no visual vocabulary, so for a masked region the model predicts the detector's class distribution for that region, a soft target, because regions can be large, and the surrounding regions and text rarely hold enough information to reconstruct their pixels. For alignment, the image token and class token outputs are multiplied element by element and fed to a binary match classifier. Multiplication amplifies features strong in both, a bit like a logical AND, which captures alignment better than addition.

ViLBERT significantly beat the state of the art on image grounding, caption-based image retrieval, even zero-shot, visual question answering, and visual commonsense reasoning. Visual commonsense reasoning means answering a multiple-choice question about an image, then choosing the right justification. Given a picture of a waiter serving pancakes, and asked why person four is pointing at person one, the model must answer that he is telling person three that person one ordered the pancakes, and justify it: person three is serving food and might not know whose order is whose. ViLBERT was influential for its dual-stream design, for co-attention, and as another demonstration of large-scale self-supervised pretraining.

CLIP. OpenAI released CLIP, contrastive language-image pretraining, in January 2021. It learns to encode text and images into vectors that are similar when they match and dissimilar when they do not.

A trained CLIP does zero-shot image classification. For an insect classifier, encode each class name, such as cricket, ladybug and spider, then encode an image and pick the class with the highest cosine similarity. Text resembling web captions works better, such as this is a photo of a ladybug, so trying various prompt templates, which is prompt engineering, helps. The Transformers library has a zero-shot image classification pipeline that uses that template by default. On a photo of a ladybug on a dandelion, it picks ladybug with over ninety-nine percent confidence. Swap the labels for flower names and the same model picks dandelion.

The architecture is just a text encoder and a vision encoder, with no co-attention. The authors tried ResNet and ViT models for vision and a GPT-2-like model for text, all trained from scratch. GPT-2 is decoder-only, but since it is not doing next token prediction here, its last token's output can represent the whole text. GPT-2 was likely chosen because OpenAI built GPT and the same lead author, Alec Radford, worked on both. A causal text encoder also allows the model to cache shared prefixes such as this is a photo of a. A pooling layer turns the image encoder's output into one vector, and a linear layer on each encoder projects both into the same space.

CLIP was pretrained on four hundred million image-caption pairs from the internet, with batches of thirty-two thousand seven hundred sixty-eight pairs. All vectors are normalized to unit length, since only direction matters. The cosine similarity between every image and every caption in the batch forms a square matrix of scores between minus one and one. The diagonal holds the matching pairs, whose scores should be near one. All other scores should be near zero, not minus one, because unrelated vectors in high dimensions are nearly orthogonal: mismatched pairs are unrelated, not opposite.

Each row is treated as a classification problem: the scores are logits, the correct class is the matching caption, and the cross-entropy loss is computed. The same is done for each column, and the final loss is the mean. The scores are first divided by a trainable temperature to get a better logit scale. This loss is known as InfoNCE. It needs very large batches so the model sees enough negative examples, and CLIP's gigantic batch size is part of its success.

On about sixty percent of image classification datasets tested, zero-shot CLIP beat a linear classifier trained on features from a frozen ResNet-50, including on ImageNet, which that ResNet-50 had been trained on. CLIP is strongest on everyday scenes with few examples per class, and even beat the state of the art on the Stanford Cars dataset, since cars are common on the web. It is weaker on domain-specific images, such as satellite or medical images.

CLIP's visual features are robust to perturbations, which suits image retrieval: index images in a vector database by their CLIP features, then search with either a text or an image query. To get features directly, the CLIP model and processor are used instead of the pipeline. Its embeddings are already normalized, so a single matrix multiplication gives all the cosine similarities. Rescaling them by the learned temperature and applying a softmax reproduces the pipeline's probabilities.

DALL-E. OpenAI announced DALL-E in February 2021. Its name blends the painter Salvador Dali and the Pixar character Wall-E. It generates images from prompts such as an armchair in the shape of an avocado. It is a GPT-like next token predictor trained on millions of image-caption pairs, each turned into text tokens followed by visual tokens from a discrete variational autoencoder. Given only text tokens, it generates visual tokens one by one until the image is complete. The model was never released, but open replications exist, such as DALL-E mini, also called Craiyon.

DALL-E 2, from April 2022, works differently: CLIP turns the text into an embedding that guides a diffusion model. It is available through a paid API and products such as Bing Image Creator and ChatGPT. DALL-E 3, from October 2023, came with no paper, code, weights or data. It is diffusion-based, does not use CLIP, and relies on GPT-4 to rewrite prompts. It follows compositional prompts with many elements far better than earlier versions, which usually honored only one or two of them.

The Perceiver. Every model so far chops inputs into meaningful tokens: subwords, image patches, video clips. Feeding raw characters, pixels or audio samples would make sequences far too long for quadratic attention. Tokenization also adds inductive biases, such as patches assuming that nearby pixels are related. But tokenization is modality-specific, which makes new modalities and mixtures harder, and with a large dataset, models with fewer assumptions often do better and find patterns that would otherwise be missed.

DeepMind's Perceiver, from March 2021, handles any modality at the lowest level, such as characters, pixels or audio samples, with a modality-agnostic design. For an image, each pixel's three color values are concatenated with Fourier positional encodings. These resemble the original Transformer's sinusoidal encodings, but cover every input dimension, so both pixel coordinates. Each coordinate, normalized between minus one and one, is encoded as itself plus the sine and cosine of pi times a frequency times the coordinate, at typically six frequencies spaced evenly from one up to half the target resolution. The limit is half because, by the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, higher frequencies add no information and can cause aliasing. Two coordinates give twenty-six positional values, so each pixel token has twenty-nine dimensions, and a linear layer projects it to the model's size, for example five hundred twelve.

The model is a stack of processing blocks, for example eight, each a single cross-attention layer followed by a regular transformer encoder, for example with six layers. Its main input is a short sequence of learned latent tokens, for example five hundred twelve. The pixel tokens enter only through the cross-attention layers, as keys and values. The latent tokens act like an RNN's hidden state, updated block after block as they gather information from the pixels. Because the latent sequence is short, the quadratic problem disappears. This is the latent bottleneck trick, the key to the Perceiver. The authors also tried sharing weights across blocks, except the first cross-attention layer, with good results; with shared weights, the Perceiver is effectively a recurrent network, and the latents really are its hidden state. The final block averages the latents into one vector for a classification head. The idea echoes the manifold hypothesis: high-dimensional data lies near a low-dimensional surface, and the Perceiver tries to work in that low-dimensional latent space.

The savings are large. A two hundred twenty-four pixel square image has about fifty thousand pixels. Self-attention over them would compute about two and a half billion scores. Cross-attention to five hundred twelve latents needs about twenty-six million, roughly a hundred times less. The Perceiver scales linearly with input size.

With the same architecture, the Perceiver was trained on images, on audio plus video, and on three-dimensional point clouds, with competitive and sometimes state-of-the-art results. For the AudioSet videos, feeding every pixel and audio sample of a ten second clip would mean about twelve and a half million pixel tokens and four hundred eighty thousand audio tokens, so the authors compromised. They trained on clips of thirty-two frames, cut into patches of two frames by eight by eight pixels, giving about twelve and a half thousand video tokens, and cut the audio into chunks of one hundred twenty-eight samples, giving four hundred eighty audio tokens. The two sequences were concatenated, with a modality embedding to tell them apart. Feeding raw audio instead of a mel spectrogram made little difference, which shows the Perceiver extracts useful features from raw data. Its limitation is that it only does classification.

---

Perceiver IO. DeepMind's Perceiver IO, from July 2021, keeps the Perceiver up to the latent tokens but replaces the pooling and classification head with a flexible output mechanism. Besides classification, it beat BERT at masked language modeling, set a new state of the art on optical flow, which is predicting where each pixel moves in the next video frame, and even played StarCraft II.

A new cross-attention layer acts as a decoder: output query tokens attend to the latent tokens, and the results go to task-specific heads. The queries depend on the task. Classification needs one learned query. Masked language modeling uses one query per masked token, namely the learnable positional embedding of that token's position: for the dog, mask, the, mask, the queries are the positional embeddings for positions two and four, and a linear and softmax head predicts each masked token. This works for any modality, with a modality embedding added when several are mixed. Optical flow uses one query per pixel. Because the queries only attend to the latents, the model scales linearly in both inputs and outputs.

Perceiver IO has no causal masking, so it is poorly suited to text generation, since it cannot cache past states the way a causal model does. Perceiver AR, from Google and DeepMind in 2022, fixes this: the last input tokens serve as latents, attention is causal over them, and training uses next token prediction. It had less impact than the Perceiver and Perceiver IO, but did very well on very long sequences, thanks to linear scaling.

Flamingo. DeepMind's Flamingo, from April 2022, is a visual-language model, or VLM, that takes any interleaved sequence of text and images and generates free-form text. Its few-shot performance is excellent, with no fine-tuning. Show it a few pictures paired with poems, then a new picture, and it writes a poem about it. Show it a few car photos with their license plate numbers, and it reads the plate on a new photo. It also captions images, answers questions about them, compares images, and describes actions in video frames.

Flamingo connects two large frozen pretrained models: a CLIP-style vision encoder and the Chinchilla language model. Each image goes through the vision encoder and then a Perceiver called the Resampler, which outputs a short, fixed number of latent tokens, avoiding the quadratic attention problem.

These tokens feed gated cross-attention dense modules inserted before every block of the frozen language model. Each module is a masked cross-attention layer and a feedforward module, each with a skip connection, and each followed by a tanh gate: its output is multiplied by the hyperbolic tangent of a learnable scalar that starts at zero. Since the tanh of zero is zero, every gate starts closed, the modules have no effect, and the language model behaves as before. During training, the gates gradually open and let visual information in.

Each text token can only attend to the visual tokens of the closest preceding image, though it reaches earlier images indirectly through the language model's own self-attention. New special tokens with trainable embeddings mark each image and the end of each image and text chunk.

Flamingo was not released, but open versions exist. OpenFlamingo, from the nonprofit LAION, is fully open source. IDEFICS, from Hugging Face, was trained on OBELICS, one hundred forty-one million interleaved text and image documents from Common Crawl, and makes it easier to swap in different language models or vision encoders; its first and third versions build on Llama, which limits some commercial use, while the second builds on Mistral, which is fully open source. Nvidia's AudioFlamingo handles audio instead of images, and domain-specific variants exist, such as Med-Flamingo, trained on medical documents.

BLIP and BLIP-2. Salesforce released BLIP, bootstrapping language-image pretraining, in January 2022. It is a mixture of encoder-decoder: a text encoder, a vision encoder, an image-grounded text encoder and an image-grounded text decoder, sharing many layers. It trains on three objectives at once: image-text matching, an image-text contrastive loss like CLIP's, and language modeling to generate captions. It was also pretrained on a large, clean dataset built with CapFilt: a captioner generated synthetic captions and a filter removed noisy ones. The final model was then trained on the cleaned dataset. This two-stage process is the bootstrapping in the name.

BLIP-2, from January 2023, reuses two large frozen models, one for vision and one for language, and outperformed Flamingo with a much smaller model. It trains in two stages.

Stage one trains the Q-Former, or querying transformer. It has the BERT-base architecture and weights, plus new cross-attention layers in every other block that attend to the frozen vision encoder's output. It processes text tokens, visual tokens, and trainable latent tokens called query tokens, whose outputs will later query the language model.

The Q-Former trains on BLIP's three objectives, each with its own attention mask. For image-text matching, query and text tokens see each other, and the averaged match logits of the query tokens are trained with binary cross-entropy. Negative captions are chosen by hard negative mining: for a chimpanzee photo, a gorilla is a more likely negative than a spacecraft. For the contrastive objective, query and text tokens cannot see each other, and the best match between any query output and the text's class token is pushed toward one for matching pairs and toward zero otherwise, as in CLIP. For language modeling, text tokens see the query tokens and earlier text tokens, and predict the next caption token. A special decode token replaces the class token so the Q-Former knows to generate rather than encode.

After stage one, the Q-Former already encodes images and text into the same space, so a chimpanzee photo and the caption a photo of a chimpanzee get very similar representations, and its query outputs are trained to be useful for next token prediction.

Stage two keeps the vision encoder and the Q-Former, drops the rest, and adds a new, randomly initialized linear layer that maps the query outputs to visual query tokens. These are concatenated with the caption's text tokens and fed to the frozen language model, and BLIP-2 is trained to predict the next caption token. This teaches the linear layer to map visual information into the language model's input space. The result generates text grounded in the image. In the book's example, a BLIP-2 model with an OPT language model, loaded with the Transformers library, captions a photo as two cats laying on a couch. InstructBLIP adds vision-language instruction tuning.

Other multimodal models. Many others exist: LayoutLM for document understanding, GLIP for visual grounding and detection, Stable Diffusion for text-to-image, PaLI and PaliGemma for multilingual vision-language tasks, PaLM-E for robot control through generated text commands, LLaVA among the best open vision-language chat models, ImageBind which extends CLIP to six modalities, SeamlessM4T for speech and text translation across about one hundred languages, Qwen-VL as a strong open multimodal family, and EMO, which animates a photo of a person to match an audio recording. Commercial models such as GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Sora, Veo-3 and Claude 4 Opus have undisclosed architectures and are used through apps or APIs. The book shows a short example sending a photo and a question to Gemini through Google's API.

Summary.

Vision transformers grew out of attention for image captioning and CNN-transformer hybrids such as DETR. The original ViT removed the CNN: it splits an image into sixteen by sixteen patches, projects them, adds positional embeddings and a class token, and runs a standard encoder. It needs very large datasets because it has fewer inductive biases than a CNN. DeiT reaches strong results without extra data by distilling from a CNN teacher through a distillation token.

Hierarchical vision transformers produce multiscale feature maps for dense prediction. PVT uses small patches and spatial reduction attention, which shrinks the keys and values but not the queries. Swin attends within local windows and shifts them every other layer, so its cost grows linearly with image area. DINO trains a student to match a momentum teacher on different views of an image, with centering and sharpening to prevent collapse, and its attention maps find objects without labels. BEiT, masked autoencoders and JEPA are other self-supervised approaches.

Multimodal learning must solve fusion and alignment, and transformers handle both, because almost anything can be tokenized and attention relates tokens across modalities. VideoBERT turns video clips into discrete tokens with hierarchical k-means. ViLBERT uses two streams joined by co-attention. CLIP trains separate image and text encoders with a contrastive loss over huge batches, enabling zero-shot classification and image search. DALL-E generates visual tokens from text, and later versions use diffusion.

The Perceiver ingests raw inputs of any modality through cross-attention into a short latent sequence, scaling linearly with input size, and Perceiver IO adds output queries for almost any output. Flamingo and BLIP-2 connect a frozen vision model to a frozen language model, through a Resampler with gated cross-attention, or through a Q-Former trained in two stages, producing models that can talk about images.
